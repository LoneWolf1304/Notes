\subsection{Spinor Representation of Lorentz group}
We know that any representation of the Lorentz group, say $\mathrm{S}[\Lambda]$ can be written in terms of the rotation generators $\vb{J}$ and the boost generators $\vb{K}$ (from \ref{sec:appA}):
$$\mathrm{S}[\Lambda] = \exp \qty(-i\ \boldsymbol{\theta}\cdot \vb{J} + i\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot \vb{K})$$
where $\bs{\theta}$ and $\bs{\eta}$ are the rotation and boost parameters. The normal boost and rotation generators are not closed under commutation, however if we define\footnote{This is called \textit{complexification}}:
$$\vb{J}^{\pm} := \frac{\vb{J}\pm i \vb{K}}{2}$$
Then, one can check that the Lorentz algebra becomes:
\begin{align*}
    \comtr{J^{+,i}}{J^{+,j}}&=i\vep^{ijk}J^{+,k}\\
    \comtr{J^{-,i}}{J^{-,j}}&=\vep^{ijk}J^{-,k}\\
      \comtr{J^{+,i}}{J^{-,j}}&=0
\end{align*}
Now, this is familiar to the $SU(2)$ algebra that we had seen from, say the \textit{angular momentum}. In fact, from the complexification of the generators we see two copies of the angular momentum algebra (so we can write $\mathfrak{so}(1,3) = \mathfrak{su}(2) \oplus \mathfrak{su}(2) $) and we can thus label the representations by $(j_-, j_+)$ where $j_{\pm}\in \{0,\frac{1}{2}, 1, \frac{3}{2}\ldots\}$. We will briefly focus on the most popular representations, namely:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Scalar representation } $\longrightarrow \qty(0,0)$
     \item \textit{Left-handed Weyl spinor representation } $\longrightarrow \qty(\sfrac{1}{2},0)$
  \item \textit{Right-handed Weyl spinor representation } $\longrightarrow \qty(0,\sfrac{1}{2})$
  \item \textit{Dirac spinor representation } $\longrightarrow \qty(0,\sfrac{1}{2})\oplus \qty(\sfrac{1}{2},0)$
\end{itemize}
The scalar representation is the trivial one-dimensional representation where $\vb{J}^{\pm} = 0 \implies \vb{J}=0, \vb{K} = 0$ and hence is of not much interest to us. All but the Dirac representation are irreducible representations. Let us now focus on the Weyl representation. \\[0.2cm]
\underline{Weyl Representation}\\[0.2cm]
Let us consider the left handed spinor representation. The left-handed spinor itself can be denoted by $\psi_L$ while any representation of the Lorentz group can be represented by $\Lambda_L$. In this case, 
$\vb{J}^{+} =0$ while $\vb{J}^{-}$ resembles the representation of spin-half particles and can be taken to be $\vb{J}^{-}\to \frac{\bs{\sigma}}{2}$ where $\bs{\sigma}$ is the Pauli vector. Then from the definition, 
\begin{align*}
    \vb{J} = \vb{J}^+ + \vb{J}^{-} = 0 + \frac{\bs{\sigma}}{2} = \frac{\bs{\sigma}}{2}\\
    \vb{K} = -i ( \vb{J}^+ - \vb{J}^{-}) = i  \frac{\bs{\sigma}}{2}
\end{align*}
Note that, the Pauli vector is Hermitian and then so is $\vb{J}$ but  $\vb{K}$ is anti-Hermitian instead, so overall the Weyl representation is not a unitary representation. This is explained by the fact that the Lorentz group is not a \textit{compact} group and by a theorem, any non-trivial finite dimensional irreducible representation cannot be unitary. \\[0.2cm]
Thus, the representation of the Lorentz group and the transformation of a left-handed Weyl spinor becomes:
$$\mathrm{S}[\Lambda_L] = \exp \qty((-i\bs{\theta} -\bs{\eta} )\cdot \frac{\bs{\sigma}}{2})\qquad \psi_L \to \Lambda_L\psi_L $$
Using a similar argument, we can see that the right-handed Weyl spinor is characterised by:
$$\mathrm{S}[\Lambda_R] = \exp \qty((-i\bs{\theta} +\bs{\eta} )\cdot \frac{\bs{\sigma}}{2})\qquad \psi_R \to \Lambda_R\psi_R $$
Now, let us come to the Dirac representation which is a reducible representation and can be written as\footnote{We will denote Dirac spinors with capital letters, that is, $\Psi$ while Weyl spinors with small letters, $\psi$ to distinguish between them, since these are inequivalent representations. Of course, it might not always be possible to remember this and the notation might slip sometimes \emoji{crying-face}}:
$$\Lambda_D \equiv \mqty(\Lambda_L & \\ & \Lambda_R)\qquad \Psi \to \Lambda_D \Psi$$
Consider an infinitesimal transformation of the Dirac representation, which can be equivalently written as the infinitesimal transformation of the two Weyl representations. 
\begin{align*}
  \exp \qty((-i\bs{\theta} -\bs{\eta} )\cdot \frac{\bs{\sigma}}{2}) &\approx \mathds{1} + \qty((-i\bs{\theta} -\bs{\eta} )\cdot \frac{\bs{\sigma}}{2}) = \mathds{1} -\frac{i}{2}\theta^i \sigma^i -\frac{1}{2}\eta^i\sigma^i\\
   \exp \qty((-i\bs{\theta} +\bs{\eta} )\cdot \frac{\bs{\sigma}}{2}) &\approx \mathds{1} + \qty((-i\bs{\theta} +\bs{\eta} )\cdot \frac{\bs{\sigma}}{2})=\mathds{1} -\frac{i}{2}\theta^i \sigma^i +\frac{1}{2}\eta^i\sigma^i
\end{align*}

Substituting this in the Dirac representation, we obtain:
\begin{align*}
  \Lambda_D &= \mathds{1} -\frac{i}{2}\theta^i \mqty(\sigma^i & \\ & \sigma^i) -\frac{1}{2}\eta^i\mqty(\sigma^i & \\ & -\sigma^i)\\
  &=\mathds{1} -\frac{i}{4}\vep^{ijk}\Omega_{jk} \mqty(\sigma^i & \\ & \sigma^i) -\frac{1}{2}\Omega^{i0}\mqty(\sigma^i & \\ & -\sigma^i)\\
  &=\mathds{1} -\frac{i}{4}\Omega_{jk} \Sigma^{jk}-\frac{i}{2}\Omega^{i0}\Sigma^{0i}\\
  &=\mathds{1} -\frac{i}{4}\Omega_{jk} \Sigma^{jk}+\frac{i}{2}\Omega_{i0}\Sigma^{0i}\\
  &=\mathds{1} -\frac{i}{4}\Omega_{jk} \Sigma^{jk}-\frac{i}{2}\Omega_{0i}\Sigma^{0i}\\
  &=\mathds{1} -\frac{i}{4}\Omega_{jk} \Sigma^{jk}-\frac{i}{4}\Omega_{0i}\Sigma^{0i}-\frac{i}{4}\Omega_{i0}\Sigma^{i0}\\
  &=\mathds{1} - \frac{i}{4}\Omega_{\rho\sigma}\Sigma^{\rho\sigma}
\end{align*}
where we have used the form of the matrix $\Sigma^{\mu\nu}$ in the chiral basis\footnote{Only $\gamma^0$ has a different form in the chiral basis.$$\gamma^0 = \mqty(0 & \mathds{1}\\ \mathds{1} & 0)$$} (since we are working with Weyl representation where the representations $\Lambda$ act on the Weyl spinors, the chiral basis is an appropriate choice). We had previously found the form of $\Sigma^{jk}$ in \ref{sec:heli} which will not change in the chiral basis. We now just find the form of $\Sigma^{0i}$:
\begin{align*}
   -2i \Sigma^{0i} &= \beta \gamma^i - \gamma^i \beta\\
    &=\mqty(0 & \mathds{1}\\ \mathds{1}&0) \gami{i} - \gami{i} \mqty(0 & \mathds{1}\\  \mathds{1} &0)\\
    &=  \mqty(-\sigma_i & 0\\0 &\sigma_i) - \mqty(\sigma_i & 0\\0 &-\sigma_i)\\
    &=-2 \mqty(\sigma_i & 0\\0 &-\sigma_i)
\end{align*}
Thus, we found an expression for the infinitesimal transformation of the spinor generators. Hence, any Dirac spinor representation of the Lorentz group can be found by exponentiating these generators:
$$\mathrm{S}[\Lambda ]= \exp \qty(-\frac{i}{4}\Omega_{\rho\sigma}\Sigma^{\rho\sigma})$$
As specified earlier, $\mathrm{S}[\Lambda]$ cannot be unitary in general, since Lorentz group is not a compact group and as a result of which, the obvious choice for the inner product of a Dirac spinor is not \textit{Lorentz invariance}. We can explicitly see this as,
\begin{align*}
  \Psi'^\dagger \Psi' =\Psi^\dagger\ \underbrace{\mathrm{S}[\Lambda]^\dagger   \mathrm{S}[\Lambda] }_{\neq \mathds{1}}\ \Psi \neq \Psi^\dagger \Psi
\end{align*}
\subsection{Constructing Bilinears}
We saw that the obvious notion of an \textit{adjoint} is not Lorentz invariant which is problematic. We will now try to construct some tensorial quantitites out of the spinors in a different way, which will be Lorentz invariant/covariant. For that, we need to look at some properties. \\[0.2cm]
Firstly note that $\gamma^i$'s are anti-Hermitian while $\gamma^0$ is Hermitian. Then we have:
$$\gamma^0 \gamma^i \gamma^0 = (-\gamma^0)^2\gamma^i = -\gamma^i = {\gamma^i}^\dagger\qquad\quad \gamma^0 \gamma^0 \gamma^0= \gamma^0={\gamma^0}^\dagger$$
Hence we have the identity:
$$\boxed{\gamma^0 \gamma^\mu \gamma^0 = {\gamma^\mu}^\dagger}$$
Now, we will try to see how the generators transform under the adjoint. For that, note:
\begin{align*}
  {\Sigma^{\mu\nu}}^\dagger &= \qty(\frac{i}{2} \comtr{\gamma^\mu}{\gamma^\nu})^\dagger\\
  &=-\frac{i}{2}\comtr{{\gamma^\nu}^\dagger}{{\gamma^\mu}^\dagger}\\
  &=-\frac{i}{2}\qty{(\gamma^0 \gamma^\nu \gamma^0)(\gamma^0 \gamma^\mu \gamma^0)-(\gamma^0 \gamma^\mu \gamma^0)(\gamma^0 \gamma^\nu \gamma^0) }\\
  &=-\frac{i}{2}\gamma^0\qty{\gamma^\nu \gamma^\mu - \gamma^\mu \gamma^\nu  }\gamma^0\\
  &= -\frac{i}{2}\gamma^0 \comtr{\gamma^\nu}{\gamma^\mu}\gamma^0\\
  &=-\gamma^0 \Sigma^{\nu\mu}\gamma^0 \\
  &=\gamma^0 \Sigma^{\mu\nu}\gamma^0
\end{align*}
Now note the property of the generators:
\begin{align*}
  \gamma^0 \underbrace{\qty(\mathcolor{red}{-}\frac{i}{4}\Omega_{\rho\sigma}\Sigma^{\rho\sigma})}_{\text{generator of $\lrep$}}\gamma^0 = \frac{i}{4}\Omega_{\rho\sigma}\gamma^0 {\Sigma^{\rho\sigma}}^\dagger \gamma^0 =\mathcolor{red}{+} \underbrace{\frac{i}{4}\Omega_{\rho\sigma}\Sigma^{\rho\sigma}}_{\text{generator of $\mathrm{S}^{-1}[\Lambda]$}} \qquad \text{from the previous property}
\end{align*}
Translating this to the actual Lorentz representations, we obtain:
$$\gamma^0\ \mathrm{S}^\dagger[\Lambda] \ \gamma^0 = \mathrm{S}^{-1}[\Lambda]\ \longleftrightarrow  \mathrm{S}^\dagger[\Lambda]  =\gamma^0\ \mathrm{S}^{-1}[\Lambda]\ \gamma^0$$
From this, we can actually define a new kind of adjoint:
$$\dadj{\Psi} := \Psi^\dagger \gamma^0$$
Let us now check how this quantity transforms under a Lorentz transformation 
$$\dadj{\Psi}(x) \longrightarrow \dadj{\Psi}'(x') = (\lrep \Psi(x))^\dagger \gamma^0 = \Psi^\dagger(x)\lrepd \gamma^0= \Psi^\dagger(x) \gamma^0 \lrepinv =\dadj{\Psi} \ \lrepinv $$
Hence this \textit{Dirac adjoint} transforms with a copy of $\lrepinv$. Using these transformations, we can construction a few tensorial quantitites. For that, remember the basis $\Gamma$ that we had constructed for $4\times 4$ matrices. Let us construct what we call \textit{bilinears}, as $\dadj{\Psi} \ (\ \cdot\ ) \ \Psi$. These are called so since these are linear in both $\Psi$ and $\dadj{\Psi}$. 
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Basis element}: $\mathds{1}$ 
  $$\bili{\mathds{1}} = \dadj{\Psi}\Psi \longrightarrow \dadj{\Psi}'\Psi' =\dadj{\Psi}\ \lrepinv \ \lrep \Psi = \dadj{\Psi}\Psi$$
  Thus, this quantity is a Lorentz \textit{scalar} since it is invariant under transformation. We denote it by $\Ss$.
  \item \textbf{Basis element}: $\gamma^\mu$ 
  \begin{align*}
    \bili{\gamma^\mu} \longrightarrow \dadj{\Psi}'\gamma^\mu\Psi' &= \dadj{\Psi}\ \lrepinv \gamma^\mu \lrep \ \Psi \\
    &=\dadj{\Psi}\ \trmat{\mu}{\nu} \gamma^\nu \ \Psi \quad \text{(using Eq. \ref{eqn:gamtrans})}\\
    &=\trmat{\mu}{\nu} \qty(\dadj{\Psi}\ \gamma^\nu \ \Psi)
  \end{align*} 
  This quantity transform with one copy of $\trmat{\mu}{\nu}$ and hence is a \textit{contravariant vector}. We denote it by $\SV$
  \item \textbf{Basis element}: $\Sigma^{\mu\nu}$
  \begin{align*}
    \bili{\Sigma^{\mu\nu}} \longrightarrow \dadj{\Psi}'\ \Sigma^{\mu\nu} \ \Psi' &= \dadj{\Psi}\ \lrepinv \Sigma^{\mu\nu}\lrep \ \Psi\\
    &=\frac{i}{2} \dadj{\Psi}\ \lrepinv \comtr{\gamma^\mu}{\gamma^\nu} \lrep\Psi\\
    &=\frac{i}{2}\qty{ \dadj{\Psi}\ \lrepinv {\gamma^\mu}{\gamma^\nu} \lrep\Psi - \dadj{\Psi}\ \lrepinv {\gamma^\nu}{\gamma^\mu} \lrep\Psi}\\
    &=\frac{i}{2}\qty{ \dadj{\Psi}\ \qty(\lrepinv {\gamma^\mu}\lrep)\qty(\lrepinv{\gamma^\nu} \lrep)\Psi - \dadj{\Psi}\ \qty(\lrepinv {\gamma^\nu}\lrep)\qty(\lrepinv{\gamma^\mu} \lrep)\Psi}\\
    &=\frac{i}{2}\qty{\dadj{\Psi} \qty(\trmat{\mu}{\alpha}\gamma^\alpha \trmat{\nu}{\beta}\gamma^\beta -\trmat{\nu}{\beta}\gamma^\beta \trmat{\mu}{\alpha} \gamma^\alpha)\Psi}\\
    &=\trmat{\mu}{\alpha} \trmat{\nu}{\beta} \frac{i}{2}\dadj{\Psi}\comtr{\gamma^\alpha}{\gamma^\beta}\Psi\\
    &=\trmat{\mu}{\alpha} \trmat{\nu}{\beta} \qty(\dadj{\Psi}\ \Sigma^{\alpha\beta}\ \Psi)
  \end{align*}
  This quantity transforms with two copies of $\Lambda$ and hence, acts as a rank-2 contravariant tensor. We denote it by $\ST^{\mu\nu}$
  \item  \textbf{Basis element}: $\gamma^5$\\[0.2cm]
  $\gamma^5$ is defined in the following way using the Levi-Civita tensor:
  $$\gamma^5 = i \gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3 = -\frac{i}{4!}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\lambda\delta}\gamma^\alpha\gamma^\beta\gamma^\lambda\gamma^\delta$$
\begin{align*}
  \bili{\gamma^5} =  \longrightarrow \dadj{\Psi}'\ \gamma^5 \ \Psi' &= \dadj{\Psi}\ \lrepinv \gamma^5\lrep \ \Psi = -\frac{i}{4!}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\lambda\delta}  \dadj{\Psi}\ \lrepinv \ \gamma^\alpha\gamma^\beta\gamma^\lambda\gamma^\delta \lrep\ \Psi
\end{align*}
Now, what we did earlier, by introducing $\lrep \lrepinv$ between each Dirac matrices, we can introduce a copy of $\Lambda$. Doing this, we obtain:
\begin{align*}
  \dadj{\Psi}'\ \gamma^5 \ \Psi' &=-\frac{i}{4!}\ \dadj{\Psi} \varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\lambda\delta} \trmat{\alpha}{\mu}\trmat{\beta}{\nu}\trmat{\lambda}{\rho}\trmat{\delta}{\theta}\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\theta \ \Psi
\end{align*}
\begin{ffact}
  The determinant of a square $n\times n$ matrix can be written in terms of the Levi-Civita tensor as:
$$\det (A) = \frac{1}{n!}\varepsilon_{\mu_1 \mu_2\ldots \mu_n}\varepsilon^{\nu_1 \nu_2\ldots \nu_n} \tensor{A}{^\mu_1 _\nu_1}\tensor{A}{^\mu_2 _\nu_2}\ldots \tensor{A}{^\mu_n _\nu_n} $$
Contracting the above equation by $\varepsilon_{\nu_1 \nu_2\ldots \nu_n} $ we obtain:
$$\varepsilon_{\nu_1 \nu_2\ldots \nu_n} \det (A) = \varepsilon_{\mu_1 \mu_2\ldots \mu_n}\tensor{A}{^{\mu_1} _{\nu_1}}\tensor{A}{^{\mu_2} _{\nu_2}}\ldots \tensor{A}{^{\mu_n} _{\nu_n}}  $$
\end{ffact}
\noindent
Using the above fact for $\Lambda$, we get:
\begin{align*}
   \dadj{\Psi}'\ \gamma^5 \ \Psi' &= -\frac{i}{4!}\dadj{\Psi}\ \varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\theta}\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\theta \det(\Lambda)\ \Psi \\
   &=\det(\Lambda)(\dadj{\Psi}\ \gamma^5 \ \Psi)
\end{align*}
This quantity changes almost like a scalar but with the determinant of the transformation. For proper Lorentz element, $\det(\Lambda)=1$ and hence this is invariant, however, a minus sign is introduced for improper Lorentz transformation. Hence this is instead called a \textit{pseudo-scalar} and is denoted by $\SP$
 \item  \textbf{Basis element}: $\gamma^\mu\gamma^5$
 \begin{align*}
  \bili{\gamma^\mu\gamma^5} =  \longrightarrow \dadj{\Psi}'\ \gamma^\mu\gamma^5 \ \Psi' = \dadj{\Psi}\ \lrepinv \gamma^\mu\gamma^5\lrep \ \Psi &=\dadj{\Psi}\ (\lrepinv \gamma^\mu \lrep) (\lrepinv\gamma^5\lrep) \ \Psi \\&= \dadj{\Psi}\ \trmat{\mu}{\nu} \gamma^\nu \times \det(\Lambda) \gamma^5 \ \Psi\\
  &=\det(\Lambda)\trmat{\mu}{\nu} (\dadj{\Psi}\ \gamma^\nu\gamma^5 \ \Psi)
 \end{align*}
 This quantity transforms very close to a rank-2 tensor but with the determinant of the transformation and hence changes sign under improper Lorentz transformation. Hence it is called a \textit{pseudo-vector} or \textit{axial vector} and is denoted by $\SA$
\end{itemize}
These five bilinears, corresponding to each element of the basis of $4\times 4$ matrices, is thus useful in constructing general quantities transforming properly under the Lorentz transformation.
