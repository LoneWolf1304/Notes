\section*{Lecture 09: Noether's Theorem}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Lecture 09: Noether's Theorem}
To understand Noether's Theorem, let us first understand what symmetry actually is. Symmetry of a system can be thought of as some transformation under which the properties of the system remain invariant.

    \centering

\end{figure}
\noindent
Discrete symmetries describes non-continuous changes in the system, like \textit{parity}  (kind of spatial reflection) and \textit{time-reversal}. \textit{Internal} symmetries include cases where the fields themselves vary while \textit{space-time} symmetry describes the system when the space-time points vary. \textit{Local} symmetries are transformations allowing us to change the system differently at different spacetime points while \textit{global} symmetries act in the same way at every point.
\begin{theorem}[Noether's Theorem]
    For every continuous global symmetry there exists a conserved current $J^\mu$, that is,
    $$\partial_\mu J^\mu = 0$$
\end{theorem}
\textit{Proof.}
Note that, under any symmetry transformation, we required our equations of motion to be invariant and hence require our action $\Ss$ to be `invariant' since action is a fundamental quantity to our system from which we derived our EOM. Note that while deriving the equations of motions, we ignored the boundary term of the action. Thus, the action can vary by some arbitrary boundary term. Using this, we define a \textit{Noether symmetry} to be a transformation which keeps the action invariant upto an integral of a total divergence, that is,
$$\delta\Ss = \int\limits_{\partial\SR} d^4\vb{x} \ \partial_\mu  K^\mu\implies \delta\SL \equiv \epsilon\  \partial_\mu K^\mu$$ The implication comes from the fact that divergence should be in first order with respect to some infinitesimal variation $\epsilon$. Let us consider the case of \textit{internal symmetries} first.\\[0.2cm]
Consider a variation in the field $\phi = \phi_0+\delta \phi$. Then, 
$$\delta\SL = \pdv{\SL}{\phi}\Bigg|_{\phi_0}\delta\phi+\pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu\phi)}\Bigg|_{\phi_0}\delta(\partial_\mu\phi) =\pdv{\SL}{\phi}\Bigg|_{\phi_0}\delta\phi+\pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu\phi)}\Bigg|_{\phi_0}\partial_\mu(\delta\phi)$$
Now, suppose there exists a solution $\widetilde{\phi}$ to the equation of motion of the system. Since the above equation is valid for any $\phi_0$, it is also valid for $\widetilde{\phi}$ and hence, we get:
\begin{alignat*}{2}
    \delta \SL &= \pdv{\SL}{\phi}\Bigg|_{\widetilde{\phi}} \, \delta \phi
    + \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi)}\Bigg|_{\widetilde{\phi}} \, \partial_\mu (\delta \phi) 
    & \quad & \\[6pt]
    &= \partial_\mu \qty( \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi)} )\Bigg|_{\widetilde{\phi}} \, \delta \phi
    + \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi)}\Bigg|_{\widetilde{\phi}} \, \partial_\mu (\delta \phi) 
    & \quad & \text{(using ELEOM)} \\[6pt]
    &= \partial_\mu \qty( \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi)}\Bigg|_{\widetilde{\phi}} \, \delta \phi )
    & \quad & \text{(using product rule)}
\end{alignat*}
Let the variation of the Lagrangian be a global symmetry, denoted by, $\delta_\alpha \phi$. Under this symmetry, we want our action $\Ss$ to change atmost by a boundary term and hence we have,
$$\partial_\mu\qty(\pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu{\phi})}\Bigg|_{\widetilde{\phi}}\delta{\phi}) = \partial_\mu K^\mu\quad\implies\quad \partial_\mu\qty(\pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu{\phi})}\Bigg|_{\widetilde{\phi}}\delta{\phi} - K^\mu)=0$$
Let us define the quantity in the bracket by $J^\mu$ which is called the \textit{Noether current}. Thus, $$J^\mu := \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu{\phi})}\Bigg|_{\widetilde{\phi}}\delta{\phi} - K^\mu$$ from where we get $\partial_\mu J^\mu =0\implies \mathrm{\ Noether\ current \ is \ conserved}$.\\[0.2cm]
The above proof worked for internal symmetries where the field itself was varied. Now, let us look into the \textit{spacetime} symmetry, where the spacetime points are varied. For simplicity, we will deal with scalar fields only. Let us consider an infinitesimal coordinate transformation,
$$x'^\mu = x^\mu + \delta x^\mu$$
% Under this transformation, the field will transform as:
% $$\phi(x)\rightarrow \phi'(\vb{x}') = \phi(x^\mu) + \partial_\mu\phi \delta x^\mu\implies \delta\delta_0\phi =\partial_\mu\phi \delta x^\mu $$
The variation in the field can be given by:
$$\delta\phi =\phi'(\vb{x}')- \phi(\vb{x}) = \phi'(x'^\mu)- \phi'(x^\mu)+ \phi'(x^\mu) -\phi(x^\mu)=\delta_0\phi(x^\mu)+\phi'({x}'^\mu)-\phi'(x^\mu) $$
where we have defined $\delta_0\phi(x^\mu):=\phi'(x^\mu)-\phi(x^\mu)$. We did this to distinguish between variation of the dependent and independent quantitites. 
Using Taylor expansion, we have:
$$\phi'(x'^\mu ) = \phi'(x^\mu +\delta x^\mu) = \phi'(x^\mu) + \pdv{\phi'}{x^\mu}\delta x^\mu\implies \phi'(x'^\mu )-\phi'(x^\mu) = \partial_\mu\phi'\delta x^\mu$$
Now, $\partial_\mu\phi'\delta x^\mu \approx \partial_\mu\phi\delta x^\mu$, since if we do the Taylor expansion of $\delta_0\phi(x'^\mu)$, we would obtain a term $\partial_\nu\delta_0$ which is a variation of second order and hence, can be neglected. Thus, we obtain:
$$\phi'(x'^\mu )-\phi'(x^\mu) = \partial_\mu\phi\delta x^\mu\implies \delta\phi = \delta_0\phi(x^\mu) + \partial_\mu\phi\delta x^\mu$$
The action is not in general invariant under both changes in coordinates and fields. Indeed, the volume element (integral measure) $\dd^4 \vb{x}$ transforms as:
$$\dd^4\vb{x}' = J\  \dd^4\vb{x}$$
where $J$ is the determinant of the Jacobian matrix. Now, we find some expression for the Jacobian: 
$$x'^\mu = x^\mu + \delta x^\mu\implies \partial_\nu x'^\mu = \tsr{\delta}{\mu}{\nu}+\partial_\nu(\delta x^\mu)$$
Now, we use an identity $\det(\mathds{1}+ \epsilon A) = 1 + \epsilon \Tr A+\SO(\epsilon^2)$ on the above equation to get an approximation for the Jacobian as:
$$J= \det(\pdv{x'^\mu}{x^\nu}) = \det(\tsr{\delta}{\mu}{\nu}+\partial_\nu(\delta x^\mu))\approx (1+\partial_\nu(\delta x^\mu)) \implies \delta J =\partial_\mu(\delta x^\mu) $$
% The general variation in the Lagrangian is given by $$\delta\SL = \partial_\mu\SL \delta x^\mu +\pdv{\SL}{\phi}\var{\delta_0\phi} + \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\nu\phi)}\delta(\partial_\nu\delta_0\phi)$$ If the equation of motion is further satisfied, we would obtain the variation in Lagrangian as:
% $$\delta\SL = \partial_\mu\SL \delta x^\mu +\partial_\mu \qty(\pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu\phi)}\delta\delta_0\phi)$$
Now, we consider the variation of the action itself and we see:
\begin{align*}
    \delta \Ss &= \int \delta (\dd^4\vb{x} )\  \SL + \int \dd^4\vb{x} \ \delta \SL\\
    &=\int\dd^4\vb{x}\ \partial_\mu\delta x^\mu\SL + \int \dd^4\vb{x}\ \delta\SL\\
    &=\int \dd^4\vb{x}\ \qty(\partial_\mu\delta x^\mu\SL+ \pdv{\SL}{\phi}\delta_0\phi + \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu\phi)}\delta_0(\partial_\mu\phi)+\pdv{\SL}{x^\mu}\delta x^\mu)
\end{align*}
We had written the variation of the Lagrangian with respect to both dependent and independent variables. Now the first and last term can be written together using product rule and hence we have:
\begin{align*}
    \delta \Ss &= \int \dd^4\vb{x}\ \qty(\partial_\mu(\delta x^\mu \SL)+ \pdv{\SL}{\phi}\delta_0\phi + \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu\phi)}\partial_\mu\delta_0\phi)\\
    &=\int \dd^4\vb{x}\ \qty(\partial_\mu(\delta x^\mu \SL)+ \pdv{\SL}{\phi}\delta_0\phi + \partial_\mu\qty(\pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu\phi)}\delta_0\phi) - \partial_\mu\qty(\pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu\phi)})\delta_0\phi)\\
    &=\int \dd^4\vb{x}\  \partial_\mu\qty(\delta x^\mu \SL +\qty(\pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu\phi)}\delta_0\phi))+\delta_0\phi\qty(\pdv{\SL}{\phi} - \partial_\mu\qty(\pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu\phi)}))
\end{align*}
For our field satisfying the equation of motion, the last term drops and we have our variation of the action and this should be equal to the boundary term, hence we have:
\begin{align*}
     \partial_\mu\qty(\delta x^\mu \SL +\qty(\pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu\phi)}\delta_0\phi)) =  \partial_\mu K^\mu
\end{align*}
In the case for multiple fields, the \textit{Noether current} turns out to be:
$$J^\mu :=\pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu\phi_i)}\delta_0\phi_i +\delta x^\mu \SL  -  K^\mu\qquad\quad \partial_\mu J^\mu =0\qed$$
The above condition is called \textit{current conservation} which we had also seen earlier for internal symmetries. However, the second term, which is purely an artifact of the spacetime symmetry, did not occur there in the conserved current for internal symmetries.  Associated with each current, we can define the charge enclosed in a volume $V$ as:
$$Q_{V} := \int\limits_{V} \dd^3\vb{x} \ J^0(\vb{x},t)  = \int\limits_{V} \dd^3\vb{x} \ \rho$$
Then, we can write the conservation equation as:
$$\dv{\rho}{t} + \div \vb{J} =0\implies \dv{Q}{t} = -\int\limits_{V} \dd^3\vb{x} \ \div \vb{J} = -\int\limits_{\partial V} \vb{J}\cdot \dd\vb{S}$$
The last equality comes from Gauss' law and $\partial V$ is the boundary of the region. If we extend the volume $V$ to cover the entire space and assume that at spatial infinities, current is zero, then we have $\dv{Q}{t}=0$ and hence, current is conserved.  
In most cases, we would have $K^\mu$ to be zero (and thus, let us take it to be zero). Let us see some examples for Noether's theorem:
\subsection{Examples: Spacetime Symmetries}
Let us start with some examples of Noether's theorem where spactime symmetry is explicitly there. We will consider translations and rotations. 
\subsubsection{Translational Invariance}
Classically, we know that translational invariance leads to \textit{energy conservation}. Let us see what happens in case of fields. We thus consider an infinitesimal global translation:
$$x^\mu\rightarrow x'^\mu = x^\mu+\epsilon a^\mu$$
Then $\delta x^\mu = \epsilon a^\mu$. As calculated before, the variation in the Lagrangian is given by:
$$\delta\SL = \partial_\mu\qty(\delta x^\mu \SL + \delta_0\phi_i \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu\phi_i)})$$
Note that $\delta x^\mu = \epsilon a^\mu$ and $\delta_0\phi_i = \phi'_i(x^\mu) - \phi_i(x^\mu)$. Note that the new field at $x^\mu$ is just the previous field at $x^\mu - \epsilon a^\mu$ and hence we have from a first-order expansion that $\delta_0\phi_i = -\epsilon a^\nu \partial_\nu\phi$. Substituting this above, we obtain:
\begin{align*}
    \partial_\mu \qty(
        \delta x^\mu \SL 
        + \delta_0 \phi_i \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}
    )
    &= \partial_\mu \qty(
        \epsilon a^\mu \SL 
        - \epsilon a^\nu \partial_\nu \phi_i \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}
   ) \\[0.5em]
    &= \partial_\mu \qty(
        \epsilon a^\nu \tsr{\delta}{\mu}{\nu} \SL 
        - \epsilon a^\nu \partial_\nu \phi_i \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}
   ) \\[0.5em]
    &= -\epsilon a^\nu \partial_\mu\qty(\partial_\nu \phi_i \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}-\tsr{\delta}{\mu}{\nu}\SL)
\end{align*}
From the above, we can thus define the conserved current as:
$$\tsr{T}{\mu}{\nu} := \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}\partial_\nu \phi_i-\tsr{\delta}{\mu}{\nu}\SL$$
Now, note the following:
$$T^{\mu\nu} = g^{\alpha\nu}\tsr{T}{\mu}{\alpha} = \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}g^{\alpha\nu}\partial_\alpha \phi_i-g^{\alpha\nu}\tsr{\delta}{\mu}{\alpha}\SL = \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}\partial^\nu\phi_i-g^{\mu\nu}\SL$$
The above quantity can be shown to be a tensor and is called the \textit{energy-momentum stress tensor}. Why?\\[0.2cm]
Note that for each value of $\nu$ we have a current four vector. Let us calculate the charge for the time-translation invariance part, that is, $\nu=0$. From this, we have:
$$P^0 = \int \dd^3 \vb{x} \ T^{00} = \int \dd^3 \vb{x} \ \qty( \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_0 \phi_i)}\partial^0\phi_i-g^{00}\SL) = \int \dd^3 \vb{x} \ \qty(\pi^i \dot{\phi}_i - \SL) = \int \dd^3 \vb{x} \ \SH = H$$
We see that corresponding to the converved current from time translation invariance, we have a conserved charge $P^0 = H$. Thus, the total Hamiltonian is conserved under time translation and hence our classical intuition worked here as well!\\[0.2cm]
Now, let us see what happens for space translations, for which we consider $\nu\equiv j = 1,2,3$. Then we have:
$$P^j = \int \dd^3 \vb{x} \ T^{0j} = \int \dd^3 \vb{x} \ \qty( \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_0 \phi_i)}\partial^j\phi_i-\cancelto{0}{g^{0j}}\SL) = \int \dd^3 \vb{x}\ \qty(\pi^i \partial^j\phi_i)$$
The last thing is the $j^{\mathrm{th}}$ component of the total momentum which is conserved in this case. Thus, we see that for spatial translation invariance, the total momentum is conserved, which once again matches with the classical analog.
\subsubsection{Lorentz Invariance}
An infinitesimal Lorentz transformation (which covers both \textit{boosts} and \textit{rotations}) can be written as:
$$\tsr{\Lambda}{\mu}{\nu} =\tsr{\delta}{\mu}{\nu}+\tsr{\omega}{\mu}{\nu} $$
where $\tsr{\omega}{\mu}{\nu}$ is an infinitesimal deviation from the identity. Now, we know for any Lorentz transformation, $$\tsr{\Lambda}{\mu}{\tau}\eta^{\tau\sigma}\tsr{\Lambda}{\nu}{\sigma} = \eta^{\mu\nu} \iff \Lambda^{\mathrm{T}} \eta \Lambda = \eta$$
If we put the infinitesimal transformation in the above equation and retain only terms upto first order in $\omega$, we would have $\omega^{\mu\nu} = \omega^{\nu\mu}$, that is, the infinitesimal deviation is an anti-symmetric matrix. The spacetime coordinates transforms as:
$$x^\mu \rightarrow x'^\mu = \tsr{\Lambda}{\mu}{\nu} x^\nu = \tsr{\delta}{\mu}{\nu}x^\nu + \tsr{\omega}{\mu}{\nu} x^\nu = x^\mu + \tsr{\omega}{\mu}{\nu} x^\nu \implies \delta x^\mu = \tsr{\omega}{\mu}{\nu} x^\nu$$
And the variation in $\phi$ is given as:
\begin{align*}
    \delta_0\phi_i = \phi'(x^\mu) - \phi(x^\mu)=\phi(x^\mu - \tsr{\omega}{\mu}{\nu} x^\nu) - \phi(x^\mu) = -\tsr{\omega}{\mu}{\nu} x^\nu\partial_\mu\phi_i
\end{align*} 
Now, we are ready to put in the variation of the Lagrangian:
\begin{align*}
    \partial_\mu \qty(
        \delta x^\mu \SL 
        + \delta_0 \phi_i \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}
    )
    &= \partial_\mu \qty(
        \tsr{\omega}{\mu}{\nu} x^\nu \SL 
        -\tsr{\omega}{\sigma}{\nu} x^\nu\partial_\sigma\phi_i \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}
   ) \\[0.5em]
    &= \partial_\mu \qty(\eta^{\mu\rho}\omega_{\rho\nu} x^\nu \SL 
        -\eta^{\sigma\lambda}\omega_{\lambda\nu}  x^\nu\partial_\sigma\phi_i \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}
           ) \\[0.5em]
    &= \partial_\mu \qty(\eta^{\mu\rho}\omega_{\rho\nu} x^\nu \SL 
        -\omega_{\lambda\nu}  x^\nu\partial^\lambda\phi_i \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}
           ) \\[0.5em]
    &= \partial_\mu \qty(\eta^{\mu\rho}\omega_{\rho\nu} x^\nu \SL 
        -\omega_{\rho\nu}  x^\nu\partial^\rho\phi_i \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}
           ) \\[0.5em]
    &= \partial_\mu \qty(-\omega_{\rho\nu} x^\nu \times \qty(-\eta^{\mu\rho} \SL 
        +\partial^\rho\phi_i \pdv{\SL}{(\partial_\mu \phi_i)}
           )) \\[0.5em]
    &= \partial_\mu \qty(-\omega_{\rho\nu} x^\nu T^{\rho\mu}) \\[0.5em]
    &=\partial_\mu \qty(\omega_{\nu\rho} x^\nu T^{\rho\mu})
\end{align*}
Since the current must be conserved for all the six elements of the anti-symmetric matrix (since anti-symmetric so there are total $\frac{4\times (4-1)}{2} = 6$ independent entries), the part of $x^\nu T^{\rho\mu}$ which is anti-symmetric in $\rho$ and $\mu$ must be conserved. After antisymmetrising and considering current for each component, we have:
$$M^{\nu\mu\rho} := x^\nu T^{\mu\rho} - x^\rho T^{\mu\nu} \qquad \partial_\mu M^{\nu\mu\rho} =0 $$
Let us now see the conserved charges for this case. Corresponding to spatial rotations, we have:
\begin{align*}
    \SJ^{ij} =\int \dd^3\vb{x} \ M^{i0j} = \int \dd^3\vb{x}\ (x^i T^{0j} - x^j T^{0i})
\end{align*}
This can be related to the angular-momentum since $T^{0i}$ was related to $P^i$, the $i^{\mathrm{th}}$ component of the momentum. Hence, rotational invariance leads to the \textit{conservation of angular momentum}. Let us see what happens when we give \textit{boosts}. 
$$\SJ^{0j} =\int \dd^3\vb{x} \ M^{00j} = \int \dd^3\vb{x}\ (x^0 T^{0j} - x^j T^{00})$$
As $t=x^0$ and we know that the current $\SJ^{0j}$ must be conserved, we have:
$$\dv{\SJ^{0j}}{t} = \dv{t}\int \dd^3\vb{x}\ (x^0 T^{0j} - x^j T^{00})= \int \dd^3\vb{x}\ T^{0j} + \int \dd^3\vb{x}\ t \ \partial_t T^{0j} - \dv{t}\int \dd^3\vb{x}\ x^j T^{00}=0$$
In terms of $P^j$, the above equation can be written as:
$$P^j + t\dv{P^j}{t} -\dv{t}\int \dd^3\vb{x}\ x^j T^{00} = 0$$
Previously we had seen that $P^j$ was the conserved charge corresponding to spatial translation and hence $P^j$ is a constant, which gives us:
$$\dv{t}\int \dd^3\vb{x}\ x^j T^{00} = \SC \ \mathrm(const.)$$
The above relation can be seen as the centre of energy (or mass) of the field travels with constant velocity as $T^{00}$ can be treated as the Hamiltonian ($\approx$ energy) density. 
\subsection{Examples: Internal Symmetries}
Let us now look at internal symmetries, where the spacetime coordinates themselves do not change but the fields change due to some global transformations. 
\subsubsection{Global $\mathbf{U(1)}$ Symmetry of Complex Scalar Field}
Consider a complex scalar field $\phi$ with the following Lagrangian:
$$\SL = (\partial_\mu \phi)(\partial^\mu \phi^*) - m^2\phi\phi^*$$
Let us consider a $U(1)$ transformation \footnote{This is an \textit{internal} symmetry as spacetime points are not varied.}. Note that elements of the group $U(1)$ are represented by $e^{i\alpha}$, that is, they lie on the unit circle. Then, for an infinitesimal change $\psi\rightarrow e^{i\alpha}\psi$, $e^{i\alpha}=1 + i\alpha +\SO(\alpha^2)$\footnote{This is a \textit{global} symmetry as $\alpha$ does not depend on the spacetime coordinates} and we have:
\begin{align*}
    \phi' &= (1+i\alpha)\phi \implies \delta_\alpha\phi = i\alpha\\
    {\phi^*}' &= (1-i\alpha)\phi^* \implies \delta_\alpha\phi^* = -i\alpha
\end{align*}
Under this transformation, we see that the Lagrangian is invariant, that is,
$$\SL' = (\partial_\mu \phi')(\partial^\mu \phi'^*) - m^2\phi'\phi'^* = e^{i\alpha}e^{-i\alpha} (\partial_\mu \phi)(\partial^\mu \phi'^*) - e^{i\alpha}e^{-i\alpha}m^2\phi'\phi'^*= \SL$$
From this, we can take $K^\mu = 0$. Using the formula for the conserved current, we have:
$$J^\mu = i\alpha\phi(\partial^\mu \phi^*) -i\alpha\phi^* (\partial^\mu \phi) \sim i(\partial^\mu \phi^*\phi -\partial^\mu \phi\phi^* )$$
The converved charge is then given by the volume integral:
$$Q = \int \dd^3\vb{x}\ J^0 = -i\int \dd^3\vb{x}\ (\phi\dot{\phi}^* - \dot{\phi}\phi^*)$$
\subsubsection{Global $\mathbf{SO(N)}$ symmetry of $\mathbf{N}$-real scalars}
