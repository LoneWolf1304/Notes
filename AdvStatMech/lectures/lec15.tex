\section*{Lecture 15: Landau-Ginzburg Theory}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Lecture 15: Landau-Ginzburg Theory}
In the Landau theory, we had assumed an average value $m$ of the magnetisation for the entire system and used this as the order parameter. However, this method is too rough as it misses any spatial dependence of the order parameter. $m$ was spatially homogenous which perhaps is not what we want. We now generalise $m \to m(\vb{x})$ where $m(\vb{x})$ is now a field.\\[0.2cm]
We focus on magnetic systems only, however, this can be applied to more general systems. To see this clearly, divide the system into $N'$ different cells $\{I_1, I_2\ldots I_{N'} \}$, each with a characteristic length $\sim l$ as shown below. Here $l$ is a mesoscopic length scale, $a\ll l \ll L$ where $a$ is the lattice spacing and $L$ is the typical linear dimension of the lattice.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering 
    \input{pics/landaucells.tex}
    \caption{Coarse graining the lattice}
\end{figure}
\noindent
Let the centre each cell $I$ be $\vb{x}$ and define the local order parameter,
\begin{equation}
    m\tund{I}(\vb{x}) = \frac{1}{N\tund{I}}\sum\limits_{i\in I} \sigma_i
\end{equation}
where $N\tund{I}$ is the number of spins in cell $I$. This process is called \textit{coarse graining}, since we are defining an order parameter, averaged over $N\tund{I}$ spins in each cell. Let us now define $\SL$ in this case. At the first glance, it seems that 
\begin{equation*}
    \SL(\{m\tund{I}\}) = \sum\limits_{i}^{N'}\qty[ \frac{1}{2}a_2(T-T_c)m_i^2 + \frac{1}{4}a_4 m_i^4 - h_im_i]
\end{equation*}
We have suppressed the $\vb{x}$ parameter, since $\vb{x}$ is dependent on $i$ itself. Note that the above free energy does not take into account the cost of maintaining the local magnetisation in adjacent cells. Every $m_i$ is independent and this can lead to large difference in the order parameter in adjacent cells. We assume that the spatial variation of the local order parameter is `slow' and for this, we introduce a symmetric coupling term, 
\begin{equation}
    \SL(\{m\tund{I}\}) = \sum\limits_{i}^{N'}\qty[ \frac{1}{2}a_2(T-T_c)m_i^2 + \frac{1}{4}a_4 m_i^4 - h_im_i] +\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{ij}\SC_{ij} (m_i - m_j)^2
\end{equation}
where $\SC_{ij}$ is the short-range coupling and based on a characteristic cutoff length $R$, is defined as,
$$\SC_{ij} = \begin{cases}
    c\tund{0} & \abs{i-j}l<R \\
    0 & \abs{i-j}l>R
\end{cases}$$
There are some subtleties here though. Note that $\vb{x}$ can take only discrete values, based on the position of the cell. Hence we assume that $\frac{N'}{N}$ is small enough that $\vb{x}$ can be taken to be continuous. Moreover, within each cell, $m\tund{I}(\vb{x}) $ is quantised in terms of $\frac{1}{N\tund{I}}$. We choose $N\tund{I}$ large enough, so that $m\tund{I}(\vb{x})$ can take any value between $-1$ and $+1$. This allows us to replace $m\tund{I}(\vb{x})$ by just a continuous function $m(\vb{x})$. Let us now manipulate the coupling term now. Assume a 1D system for ease.
\begin{align}
   \begin{split}
     \frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{ij}\SC_{ij}(m_i-m_j)^2 &= \sum\limits_i \sum\limits_{j=1}^{R/l} c\tund{0} (m_{i+j}-m_i)^2 \\
    &=\sum\limits_i \sum\limits_{j=1}^{R/l} c\tund{0}\qty[ \frac{(m_{i+j}-m_i)^2}{(jl)^2}] (jl)^2\\
   \end{split}
\end{align}
The thing in the bracket looks like the discrete version of a derivative. In the continuum limit, this leads to $(\partial_x m(x))^2$, which becomes the gradient term $\qty(\grad m(\vb{x})) \cdot \qty(\grad m(\vb{x}))$ in general $d$ dimensions. The sum over $i$ can be converted to a $d$-dimensional integral,
$$\sum\limits_i \equiv \frac{1}{l^d}\int \dd^d x$$
where $l^d$ is the elemental volume in each cell with centre $\vb{x}$. The sum over $j$ is just a constant and depends on the interaction range $R$. Thus, absorbing every constants into a single parameter $\gamma$, the coupling term becomes, 
\begin{equation*}
      \frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{ij}\SC_{ij}(m_i-m_j)^2 \simeq \frac{\gamma}{2} \int \dd^d x\ \qty(\grad m(\vb{x}))^2
\end{equation*}
The final expression for the Landau free energy is,
\begin{equation}
    \SL[m(\vb{x})] = \int \dd^d x \ \qty[\frac{1}{2}a_2(T-T_c)(m(\vb{x}))^2 + \frac{1}{4}a_4 (m(\vb{x}))^4 - h(\vb{x})m(\vb{x}) +\frac{\gamma}{2}\qty(\grad m(\vb{x}))^2]
\end{equation}
Note that, $\SL$ has now become a \textit{functional}, that is, it takes a function $m(\vb{x})$ and spits out a scalar. The partition function calculation becomes a bit involved, since it contains the term $\exp(-\beta \SL[m(\vb{x})])$ and we have to perform an integral. However, this is not an ordinary integral, since the integration variable is now a function $m(\vb{x})$. What happens is that there are infinite realisations of the function $m(\vb{x})$ and we need to `integrate' over all these realisations. The process through which it is done is called \textit{functional integral} or a more cool sounding \textit{path integral}. The formal notation of the functional integral is, 
\begin{equation}
    \SZ = \int \SD m(\vb{x})\ e^{-\beta  \int \dd^d x \ \qty[\frac{1}{2}a_2(T-T_c)(m(\vb{x}))^2 + \frac{1}{4}a_4 (m(\vb{x}))^4 - h(\vb{x})m(\vb{x}) +\frac{\gamma}{2}\qty(\grad m(\vb{x}))^2]}
\end{equation}
Out of the infinite realisations of $m(\vb(x))$, the one which gives the equation of state is the one which minimises the free energy functional. For minimisation purpose, we have to invoke the concept of function derivative since $m(\vb{x})$ is a function. Thus, we have the equation of state as, 
$$m^*(\vb{x})\ : \quad \fdv{\SL}{m} = 0$$
Before formally going down this rabbit hole, let us find the minima by simply calculating the variation $\delta \SL$ when the function $m(vb{x})$ is varied from the minimising function as $m(\vb(x)) = m^*(\vb{x}) + \delta m (\vb{x})$, such that at the boundaries, $\delta m =0$.
\begin{align*}
    \delta \SL = \int \dd^dx\ \qty[a_2(T-T_c)m\delta m + a_4 m^3 \delta m - h \delta m + \frac{\gamma}{2}\times 2\qty(\delta(\grad m)\cdot (\grad m))]
\end{align*}
Let us calculate the variation of the gradient term.
\begin{align*}
    \int \dd^dx \ (\grad m ) \delta (\grad m) &=  \int \dd^dx \ (\grad m )  \grad( \delta m) \\ 
    &\stackrel{\text{\tiny IBP}}{=} - \int \dd^dx \ (\grad^2 m )  \delta m + \qty[\grad m \delta m]_{\text{bound.}}\\
    &=- \int \dd^dx \ (\grad^2 m )  \delta m
\end{align*}
The boundary term is zero, since $\delta m$ is zero at the boundaries. From the variation of the functional, we obtained 
$$\delta\SL = \int \dd^dx \ \qty[a_2(T-T_c)m(\vb{x})+a_4(m(\vb{x}))^3-h(\vb{x})-\gamma \grad^2m(\vb{x})] \delta m = 0$$
Since $\delta m$ is arbitrary, we have the equation of state satisfied by, 
$$\boxed{a_2(T-T_c)m^*(\vb{x})+a_4(m^*(\vb{x}))^3-h(\vb{x})-\gamma \grad^2m^*(\vb{x})=0}$$
